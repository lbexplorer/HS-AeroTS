\subsection{PX4 Architecture-Aware Diagnostic Evidence}

PX4 organizes flight-control functionality as interacting modules rather than as a monolithic program. Its middleware, uORB, provides typed publish--subscribe communication through which estimators, controllers, sensor drivers, and other services exchange state and command data \cite{meier2015px4,px4uorbdocs2026}. The same topic vocabulary is represented in ULog telemetry, creating a practical bridge between an influential logged channel and the software components that publish or consume its corresponding uORB topic. This bridge is useful only when firmware provenance is retained: module boundaries, topic definitions, and communication relations can change across PX4 revisions, so an architecture map built for a different version may produce misleading suspects.

The uORB graph supports several complementary interpretations of diagnostic evidence. A producer-oriented view emphasizes modules that may have generated an abnormal value; a consumer-oriented view emphasizes modules whose behavior may have been affected by that value; and a bidirectional view retains both roles when the available evidence does not justify choosing between them. Propagating topic-level importance over these relations can therefore reduce a large source tree to a reviewable set of software-module suspects. Nevertheless, this evidence is structural and version-specific. Subscription does not establish that the relevant execution path was active, publication does not establish that the produced value was erroneous, and high-connectivity topics may implicate many modules. Architecture can narrow the inspection surface, but connectivity alone cannot identify a cause.

This setting differs from conventional software fault localization. Spectrum-based approaches rank statements, methods, or files by contrasting their execution in passing and failing tests; early visualization work demonstrated how such suspiciousness information can guide inspection, and subsequent surveys systematized a broad family of statistical ranking techniques \cite{jones2002visualization,wong2016survey}. Their evaluation normally assumes a known faulty entity and reports measures such as Top-$k$ accuracy, mean reciprocal rank, or inspection effort. Studies using seeded faults and real defects have also shown that conclusions can depend strongly on the benchmark, fault population, and evaluation protocol \cite{pearson2017evaluating,papadakis2019mutation}. Real-flight telemetry generally provides neither program-coverage spectra nor verified source-code defect labels, so applying the causal language or ground-truth assumptions of test-based fault localization would be unwarranted.

Accordingly, the present work positions commit-specific topic-to-module propagation as an architecture-aware diagnostic evidence layer, not as causal root-cause analysis. Runtime anomaly labels support evaluation of anomaly detection and fault-domain diagnosis, whereas controlled replay with known source mutations is required to evaluate software-module suspect ranking against a source-level target. Even in that setting, onset-conditioned ranking must be distinguished from detector-gated end-to-end ranking: if the runtime detector does not flag a run, the downstream localization chain produces no result. The resulting module list should thus be read as prioritized evidence for engineering inspection, conditional on the observed telemetry, the explanation model, and the reconstructed PX4 revision; flights from other revisions require their own mapping or an explicit compatibility analysis.
