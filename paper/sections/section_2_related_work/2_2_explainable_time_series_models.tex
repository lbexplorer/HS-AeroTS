\subsection{Explainable Time-Series Models}

Post-hoc attribution methods explain a model prediction by assigning relevance to its inputs, but their mechanisms and units of explanation differ. LIME fits a local surrogate around an individual prediction, Integrated Gradients accumulates input gradients along a path from a reference, and SHAP represents a prediction through additive feature contributions grounded in Shapley values \cite{ribeiro2016lime,sundararajan2017axiomatic,lundberg2017shap}. For sequential models, TimeSHAP adapts perturbation-based Shapley analysis to recurrent time-series predictions and can attribute relevance to features, events, and time steps \cite{bento2021timeshap}. These approaches make otherwise opaque predictions more inspectable, yet their immediate outputs remain tied to the model input representation. In windowed UAV telemetry, an attribution to a statistical descriptor is not automatically an explanation at the physical-channel or fault-domain level.

Tree ensembles provide a useful setting for structured attribution because TreeSHAP computes additive contributions for tree-model outputs and supports aggregation from local explanations to broader patterns \cite{lundberg2017shap,lundberg2020trees}. When each temporal-statistical feature has a fixed provenance, contributions can be grouped from descriptor to telemetry channel and then to a logged topic or functional subsystem. Hierarchical aggregation improves engineering readability, but it also introduces a bookkeeping requirement: every lower-level feature must have a defined parent, and the total evidence magnitude should be preserved when groups form a disjoint partition. A visually plausible subsystem ranking is therefore insufficient unless the aggregation can be traced back to the original model features and its numerical conservation is checked explicitly.

Explanation evaluation must also test whether attributed features are functionally important to the prediction. Sanity-check research has shown that visually appealing saliency patterns can remain insensitive to model parameters or data relationships \cite{adebayo2018sanity}. Removal-based benchmarks instead evaluate faithfulness by perturbing highly ranked inputs and measuring the resulting performance change \cite{hooker2019benchmark}. Broader evaluation frameworks, including Quantus, organize such tests into complementary properties rather than treating one score as a complete validation \cite{hedstrom2023quantus}. For time-series diagnosis, top-$k$ masking is particularly useful when it is compared with an equal-size random mask: a larger performance decrease under attribution-guided masking provides evidence that the selected features are prediction-relevant under the chosen perturbation, while the random baseline controls for the generic effect of removing inputs. Median replacement can create off-manifold samples and therefore does not prove feature necessity in an interventionist or causal sense.

Faithfulness to the predictor does not by itself establish consistency with a diagnostic label. In a structured UAV setting, the highest-ranked channels can additionally be compared with a predeclared semantic channel-to-subsystem mapping through Consistency@$K$ or Hit@$K$. This test asks whether the explanation emphasizes telemetry heuristically associated with the labeled fault domain, whereas masking asks whether the same evidence is prediction-relevant under perturbation; the two criteria are related but not interchangeable. The present framework therefore evaluates explanations at three levels: numerical conservation of hierarchical aggregation, perturbation sensitivity through attribution-guided versus random masking, and agreement with a predefined semantic mapping through top-$k$ channel matching. These tests support the interpretation of SHAP values as organized diagnostic evidence, but they do not convert feature attribution into physical causality, independent ground truth, or root-cause localization.
