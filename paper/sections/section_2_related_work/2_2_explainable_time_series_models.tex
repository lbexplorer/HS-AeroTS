\subsection{Explainable Time-Series Models}

Post-hoc attribution methods explain a model prediction by assigning relevance to its inputs, with different mechanisms and units of explanation. LIME fits a local surrogate around an individual prediction, Integrated Gradients accumulates input gradients along a path from a reference, and SHAP represents a prediction through additive feature contributions grounded in Shapley values \cite{ribeiro2016lime,sundararajan2017axiomatic,lundberg2017shap}. For sequential models, TimeSHAP adapts perturbation-based Shapley analysis to recurrent time-series predictions and can attribute relevance to features, events, and time steps \cite{bento2021timeshap}. This literature supports the use of post-hoc evidence to inspect otherwise opaque predictors. In windowed UAV telemetry, however, the immediate unit of explanation remains the model input; an attribution to a statistical descriptor must be further related to the physical channel or fault domain before it becomes engineering evidence.

Tree ensembles provide a useful setting for structured attribution because TreeSHAP computes additive contributions for tree-model outputs and supports aggregation from local explanations to broader patterns \cite{lundberg2017shap,lundberg2020trees}. When each temporal-statistical feature has fixed provenance, contributions can be grouped from descriptor to telemetry channel and then to a logged topic or functional subsystem. This hierarchy supplies the missing bridge between the representation used by the detector and the units used in flight-control diagnosis. It also introduces a bookkeeping requirement: every lower-level feature must have a defined parent, and the total evidence magnitude should be preserved when groups form a disjoint partition. HS-AeroTS therefore treats conservation as an auditable property of the mapping rather than relying on a visually plausible ranking.

Explanation evaluation must also test whether attributed features are relevant to the prediction under a specified perturbation. Sanity-check research examines whether saliency patterns respond to model parameters or data relationships, while removal-based benchmarks evaluate the effect of perturbing highly ranked inputs \cite{adebayo2018sanity,hooker2019benchmark}. Broader evaluation frameworks, including Quantus, organize such tests into complementary properties rather than treating one score as complete validation \cite{hedstrom2023quantus}. For time-series diagnosis, top-$k$ masking compared with an equal-size random mask tests whether attribution-guided removal causes a larger performance change than generic input removal. HS-AeroTS adopts this comparison together with a training-derived replacement strategy. Because median or donor replacement can create off-manifold samples, the resulting performance change indicates prediction relevance under the selected perturbation, not feature necessity or physical causality.

Faithfulness to the predictor does not by itself establish consistency with a diagnostic label. In a structured UAV setting, the highest-ranked channels can additionally be compared with a predeclared semantic channel-to-subsystem mapping through Consistency@$K$ or Hit@$K$. This test asks whether the explanation emphasizes telemetry heuristically associated with the labeled fault domain, whereas masking asks whether the same evidence is prediction-relevant under perturbation; the two criteria are related but not interchangeable. The present framework consequently evaluates explanations at three levels: numerical conservation of hierarchical aggregation, perturbation sensitivity through attribution-guided versus random masking, and agreement with a predefined semantic mapping through top-$k$ channel matching. These tests position SHAP values as organized diagnostic evidence and provide the support for the evidence layer in HS-AeroTS, without converting feature attribution into physical causality, independent ground truth, or root-cause localization.
